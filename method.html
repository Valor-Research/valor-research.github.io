Method

Every figure on this site comes from the public sources named beside it. This page explains what the site is, where its numbers come from, how they are fitted and read, and what it deliberately leaves out.

What this is

A small fixed-income reading room, kept by one person. It does two things. It redraws, every day, the government bond curves that two central institutions publish as fitted parameters — the German curve from the Bundesbank and the U.S. Treasury curve from the Federal Reserve Board — and lets you point at any tenor to see the figure and the parameters behind it. And it carries a weekly letter that describes the week in the Treasury and Bund markets in plain sentences: what moved, by how much, against which published series.

It is reporting, not advice. There is no rating, no target, no "buy" or "sell", no view on what you should do. The letter says what happened; the curves show where things stand. Readers who want an opinion will have to form their own, which is rather the point.

Where the numbers come from

The curves on this site come from the Deutsche Bundesbank (the parameters of its daily German government bond curve), the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System (the parameters of its Treasury yield curve) and the U.S. Department of the Treasury (the daily par yield curve). The anchors come from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, the European Central Bank and EIOPA.

Every chart and every table names its source and the date the data was read. Where a source asks to be cited in a particular way, it is; where a source's figures have been transformed (a par yield computed from zero-coupon parameters, say), the transformation is stated beside the result.

How the curves are fitted

Neither curve on this site is fitted here. Both institutions publish, each day, the handful of parameters of a smooth curve they have fitted to the prices of their government's bonds — the Nelson–Siegel–Svensson family, in which a level, a slope and two humps describe the whole term structure with six numbers. This site takes those published parameters and redraws the curve from them, so what you see is the institution's own fit, not an approximation of it.

From the parameters three curves follow, and the site shows all three: the zero-coupon yield (what a single payment at that date is worth today), the instantaneous forward rate (the rate the curve implies for a vanishingly short loan starting at that date — the most sensitive of the three and the one that shows the humps), and the par yield (the coupon a bond issued today at that maturity would carry to be worth exactly its face value). The par yields are computed here from the published zero-coupon curve under each market's own coupon convention — annual for Bunds, semi-annual for Treasuries — and the Treasury's own published par points are drawn beside the Board's fitted curve so that the two can be compared by eye.

The fitted parameters themselves, per date, are offered as a download, so that anyone can redraw the curves or check the arithmetic.

What the macro anchors are

A yield curve is a market price; an anchor is an estimate of where, in the long run, it ought to settle. The site shows three published anchors with their publishers' names and dates and does not add one of its own.

The neutral rate of interest — the short-term real rate that neither stimulates nor restrains the economy — as the Federal Reserve Bank of New York estimates it from the model of Holston, Laubach and Williams, updated quarterly. The long-run inflation expectation of professional forecasters, from the European Central Bank's Survey of Professional Forecasters, updated quarterly. And the ultimate forward rate that European insurance regulation uses to extend risk-free curves beyond the last liquid maturity, as EIOPA publishes it each year.

Read together, the three say roughly where a very long nominal yield "should" sit if the economy returned to its long-run state — a yardstick, not a forecast, and one whose own uncertainty the publishers describe at length in the documents linked.

How the Letter is written and audited

The weekly letter has two parts, produced in two ways.

The exhibits — the tables and charts from page two on — are produced by rule from the published series: each figure is read from its source, dated, and placed; nothing on those pages passes through a language model.

Page one is prose, and it is drafted by a language model that runs on the author's own computer — no figure leaves the machine to be written about. The model is given only the week's measured figures and a short list of the direction words the house allows ("rose", "fell", "flattened", "widened", and so on). Its draft is then audited sentence by sentence by a separate program that checks two things: that every number on page one is the exact figure in the exhibits, to the printed decimal, and that every direction word agrees with the sign of the change it describes. A sentence that fails either check is removed, not corrected. The count of sentences kept and dropped is recorded for every issue.

After the audit, a named person reads the issue and decides whether it goes out. The line at the foot of page one names who that was. Nothing is posted that has not been read.

What we deliberately do not print

Prices from licensed market feeds. Futures, options and broker-sourced figures are not reproduced here; the letter may say that a spread widened or that volatility fell, but where the underlying number comes from a licensed feed the number is left out and the sentence says so. Only figures from public institutions — treasuries, central banks, statistical agencies, regulators — appear as numbers.

Forecasts as numbers to act on. Where the author's own models produce a forecast, this site shows only the history of that forecast's errors once the forecast dates have passed — how right or wrong it was — never the current forecast itself. That is the honest way to show a model's worth without turning a page into a tip.

Ratings, targets, fair values and scores. None, for any instrument, ever. A statement of the kind "this is cheap" or "this will outperform" is a recommendation in the eyes of European market-abuse rules, and this site makes none.

The author's own positions and trades. The standing line at the foot of every page says that the author holds positions and trades for his own account; the pages never say which.

How to tell us we are wrong

Please do. Every page carries a link to a discussion board and an email address. A figure that looks wrong, a source misread, a method you would fit differently, a sentence on page one that the audit should have caught — all of it is welcome, and corrections are made in the open: a corrected issue says what was corrected. The most useful feedback is specific: the page, the figure, the date, and what you believe it should read.

The research discipline behind it

The author's own research, of which this site shows a small public corner, is run under a written discipline. Before any strategy is tested, its rules are written down and dated. Historical data is split into a design window and a sealed window that is opened only once, after the rules are fixed, so that a result cannot be tuned to the data that will judge it. A strategy earns its place by how it behaves on the sealed window and afterwards in a forward simulation on data that did not exist when it was designed — never by how it looks on the data it was built from. Most ideas fail this test, and the failures are kept on record with the same care as the successes.

None of that research is published here — the site shows public data read carefully, and a letter that describes it. But the habit of writing the rules down first, sealing the test, and counting the failures is the same one that produced the pages you are reading, and it is why the letter would rather drop a sentence than let an unaudited number through.
